Treadmill Maximal Exercise Tests

Do you want to lose fat efficiently when running? Speed and distance during your runs influence whether your body primarily uses fats or carbohydrates for fuel. This process is measured by the Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER), which is calculated as the ratio of carbon dioxide production (VCO2) to oxygen consumption (VO2). RER = VCO2 / VO2 Metabolic transition from fats to carbs for a runner occurs when they approach a respiratory exchange ratio (RER) of 1. This balance between burning carbohydrates and fat during exercise is important because it directly affects performance, endurance, recovery, and overall fitness goals. For example, you could burn carbs too early and hit a performance wall before you burn the desired amount of fat. Understanding this transition allows athletes to adjust their pacing, nutrition, and training strategies to enhance endurance and sustain peak performance.

The dataset used in this webpage is from the "Treadmill Maximal Exercise Tests from the Exercise Physiology and Human Performance Lab of the University of Malaga" [1]. The study records performance data from over 846 runners across various demographics as they incrementally increase their speeds on a treadmill. Participants include both amateur and professional athletes, ranging in age from 10 to 63 years old. Each data point is represented by a breath measurement of the runner on a treadmill.

The density histogram below shows distributions of RER across different demographics such as Age, Sex, Weight, and Height. Any combination with less than 100 data points will not be displayed on the histogram.

Population Filters

If you explored the histogram above, you can see that certain aspects, such as age and weight, shift the distribution of RER more than others, which indicates a higher correlation with the RER values. What could this mean? You may not be optimizing your run according to your body.

Below is a scatterplot that dynamically shows how RER values shift as speed and time increase, helping users see when they rely more on fat or carbohydrates for specific demographics. For each demographic bin, a linear regression model predicts the RER values throughout a run, averaging speed at certain points in time.

Enter your information and explore the plot! You can also visualize your running plan directly on the scatterplot by right-clicking and dragging your mouse to create a line and observe how the RER value changes.

Calculate Your Personal RER

If you drew lines through various demographic combinations, you likely noticed that slower and steady runs keep the RER low throughout, while faster runs make the RER higher. You can also find that for older and heavier populations, the RER value increases with more ease, meaning that to achieve a certain RER, they should adjust their workout to be slower compared to younger/lighter populations. Try out more combinations and see if you can find more patterns in the data.

The linear regression line of the scatter plot and the ability for the user to draw their own line helps visualize potential improvements users can make to their runs. This gives a complete freedom for users to explore various workouts, which we think stands out from other methods of visualization and could be a good feature that can be incorporated into a lot of visualizations.

Why does all this matter? The flexibility of customization of our visualizations help determine whether you are training effectively for endurance, performance, or fat loss, allowing personalized insights into optimizing energy use for different demographics and fitness goals.

Some limitations of these interactive visualizations however, include lack of data for some demographic combinations as well as not accounting for environmental factors such as the temperature and humidity around the runners.
